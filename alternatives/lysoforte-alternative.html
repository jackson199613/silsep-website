Lysophospholipid Emulsifiers:
The Numbers Nobody Publishes

INCLUDING, UNTIL THIS PAGE, US

This is the category where the disclosure gap is widest. Buyers are asked to choose between products on the basis of a mechanism diagram and a citation to someone else's meta-analysis, while the two numbers that would let them compare — LPC content and HLB — are absent from every public datasheet we could find.

That includes ours, partially. We publish HLB 8–12 for our LPC emulsifier. We do not publish an LPC percentage. So this page is not us pointing at an industry failing from outside it; it is us describing a norm we also participate in, and telling you what to ask so that the norm costs you less.

1. The vocabulary problem

Three words get used interchangeably in sales material and they are not the same thing:

TermWhat it actually isWhy it matters to you
LecithinCrude phospholipid mixture, usually soy-derived Weakly surface-active at feed inclusion rates — a different product class, sold at a different price
Lysolecithin Lecithin enzymatically treated (typically phospholipase A2) so one fatty acid is removed per molecule This conversion is what makes it work as an emulsifier at low inclusion
Lysophospholipid / LPC The chemical class; lysophosphatidylcholine is the species products are usually characterised by The conversion rate — how much of the lecithin actually became lyso — is the whole product

Since conversion efficiency is the core process know-how, it is also the thing least likely to be disclosed. Which means a buyer comparing two bags of powder has no direct way to tell whether one contains twice the functional material of the other.

3. What an ME uplift claim is actually worth

Suppliers in this category support their products with peer-reviewed literature rather than with a kcal/kg figure — Kemin, for instance, cites a meta-analysis of 33 trials in British Poultry Science showing improved broiler performance across varied fat levels and sources. That is a legitimate way to support a product, and better than no evidence at all.

But an averaged effect across many diets is not a number you can put in your matrix. The effect size depends on:

  • Fat source. Highly saturated fats benefit most; already-digestible oils benefit least.
  • Fat inclusion level. More fat to emulsify means more scope for improvement.
  • Species and age. Young animals with immature bile salt secretion have the most to gain.
  • Diet energy density. High-energy aqua and broiler finisher diets are where this class earns its inclusion.

If someone gives you a single kcal/kg number without asking any of the above, that number was not generated for your operation.

4. Measuring it yourself

  1. Pick the right endpoint. Feed conversion ratio against a control is what pays. Apparent fat digestibility, if you can run it, tells you whether the mechanism actually engaged.
  2. Power the trial for the effect you expect. The realistic effect is a few percent. Too few replicates and you will measure noise and conclude nothing — which is worse than not running it, because you will believe the result.
  3. Test where the mechanism should work. Highest-fat diet, youngest animals. If it does not show there, it will not show elsewhere.
  4. Hold everything else constant. Same fat source, same batch, same house. Emulsifier effects are small enough that a changed fat source will swamp them.
  5. Do not judge it on pellet quality. It is not a binder, and a better-looking pellet tells you nothing about fat digestion.

Frequently asked

Lecithin is a crude phospholipid mixture, usually from soy. Lysolecithin is lecithin that has been treated — typically enzymatically with phospholipase A2 — so that one fatty acid is removed from each phospholipid molecule. That single change is what makes it surface-active enough to work as an emulsifier at feed inclusion rates. Lysophospholipid is the broader chemical term for the resulting class; lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) is the specific species most products are characterised by. In marketing copy these words are used loosely, so ask which one is actually in the bag and at what content.

Suppliers reference peer-reviewed meta-analyses showing improved growth performance across varied fat levels and sources — Kemin cites a 33-trial meta-analysis in British Poultry Science, for example — but an averaged effect across many diets is not a number you can drop into your own matrix. The honest answer is that the uplift depends on your fat source, fat level, species and age, and the only figure that means anything for your operation is one you generate in it.

Usually less than the marketing implies. An emulsifier works on the interface between fat and the aqueous phase of the gut, so its scope for improvement scales with how much fat there is to emulsify and how hard that fat is to digest. Highly saturated fats, young animals with immature bile salt production, and high-energy aqua diets are where the effect is largest. A low-fat diet fed to mature animals is where it is smallest.

Feed conversion ratio against a control is the practical endpoint, and it needs enough replicates to detect the size of effect you are hoping for — typically a few percent, which is easy to lose in noise. Apparent fat digestibility gives you a more direct mechanistic read if you can run it. Do not judge an emulsifier on pellet quality or on how the feed looks; those are not what it is for.
